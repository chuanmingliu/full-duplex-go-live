Four channels, running at once

A cascade of speech-to-text, a language model and text-to-speech is half duplex by default: it stops listening while it speaks. This is the same cascade with that gate removed — and the machinery that removing it makes necessary. Press Run a turn, then interrupt it.

Four things to try

Interrupt

Run a turn, and cut in while speak is lit. In golive the listen lane lights on top of speak — both on, same instant. That overlap is the whole claim.

The same cut, half duplex

Now the input arrives while the floor is taken. It lands in a hatched block: audio that existed and was never heard, because the listener was switched off.

Speculation

With it on, think starts inside the hatched hangover — during the silence the VAD is still waiting out, before the turn is officially over. Clear the box and watch think slide right, past VAD close.

Prewarm

The gap between think starting and speak lighting includes a socket to the synthesis vendor. Clear prewarm to put that handshake back on the critical path, where it used to live.

Where the silence goes

The two dashed markers are the whole latency argument. Voice ends is the last frame of sound you made. VAD close is vad.min_silence_ms later — 380 ms of quiet before the engine is willing to believe you have finished, because anything shorter cuts people off mid-thought. That 380 ms is pure hangover, and it used to be dead time.

Speculation spends it. speculative_stable_ms into the pause, with the transcript unchanged throughout, the backend starts on the partial — so by the time the VAD closes the turn, the answer is already being written and, often, already being synthesized. The service reports every stage from VAD close, which is why first audio can legitimately come out negative: the first byte left before the turn officially ended.

The trigger is the pause, not a quiet recognizer, and that distinction was learned the expensive way. An earlier build fired when the partial transcript stopped changing for 260 ms — which a recognizer running behind the speaker also does, mid-sentence. It guessed on prefixes, and nearly every speculative turn was immediately revised: slower than not speculating at all, because the wasted generation still had to be cancelled.

Why interrupting forces the rest of the design

Letting someone talk over the assistant sounds like a small feature. It is not — it creates two problems that a half-duplex cascade never has to answer, and the answers are most of the engine.

Stopping four stages at once

When the cut lands, an ASR stream, a language-model stream, a synthesis socket and a playback queue are all in flight. Threading a cancellation through every provider call would be invasive and slow; instead each stage records the generation it was born in, and a single counter increments. Work from an older generation is dropped rather than raced. Watch gen in the toolbar when you interrupt — the greyed-out blocks are work that finished and was thrown away.

Knowing what was actually heard

The model wrote a whole sentence; the listener heard the first two thirds of it. If the whole sentence enters conversation history, every later turn reasons from words nobody received. So output is paced — sent at roughly real time rather than dumped into the client's buffer — which means the server can say how many milliseconds landed, and trim the transcript to that prefix. The struck-through text after a cut is what gets discarded.

What this still isn’t

A genuine full-duplex model hears prosody and decides to yield the floor from acoustics — it knows you are winding down before you stop. Here, floor control is an energy threshold and a pair of timers, and speculation infers your intent from a pause long enough to be suspicious. That shows up as barge-in detected a beat late, and as a wrong guess whenever you pause mid-thought rather than mid-turn. Both cost a cancelled generation, never a wrong answer. The gap is real, and it is the honest reason this is called a simulation.
